    Arrivals

      How it decided

      1. Crop and even out. The face is cut out, resized to 200×200 and its contrast evened out, so a dim room and a bright room look alike.
      2. Describe the texture. Each pixel is compared with its 8 neighbours: brighter or darker? Those 8 yes/no answers make a code for spots, edges and corners.
      3. Compare. The face is split into an 8×8 grid and the codes in each square are counted. Each square is compared with the enrolled photos. Brighter squares differ more. Eyes and brows count most.
      4. Decide. It must be close to one student and clearly closer to them than to anyone else, including a hidden crowd of 192 faces of people who aren't in this class,, in 5 of the last 8 frames. One bad frame (a blink, a glance down) doesn't reset it.
      The maths
      d = 1Σ w Σj wj Σb (Sjb − Mjb)²Sjb + Mjb

      S is the live face and M an enrolled photo. j runs over the 128 grid squares (64 squares at two radii) and b over the 59 texture codes. wj is how useful each square is, learned from data. A face is accepted when d ≤ τd and dclosest / dsecond ≤ τr.

      2. The Haar cascade, running on your face

      Viola–Jones: 25 stages and 2,913 rectangle tests on a 24×24 window. This re-runs OpenCV's own cascade file step by step in NumPy (it matches OpenCV's decisions on the same windows), so you can watch each test vote.

      3. Who is it? The distance race

      χ² distance from your face to every enrolled student (closest of their 60 templates) and to the closest of 192 strangers hidden in the gallery. Shorter bar = more alike.

      4. Your face's fingerprint, in 3D

      Each square of your face (8×8 grid) holds 59 bars: how often each kind of texture code (spot, edge, corner, flat…) appears there. These 3,776 numbers (and the same again at radius 2) are what gets compared, not the photo. Drag to turn it.
